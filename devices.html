Section 3 · roughly 7 to 10 marks

Electronic Devices

A small set of formulas used again and again with different numbers. Most errors here are unit errors, so work in centimetres throughout and write the units on every line.

Standard references: Neamen, Semiconductor Physics and Devices; Streetman and Banerjee, Solid State Electronic Devices. Constants at 300 K: \(kT/q=25.9\text{ mV}\), \(q=1.6\times10^{-19}\text{ C}\), \(\varepsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-14}\text{ F/cm}\), \(\varepsilon_{r,\text{Si}}=11.7\), \(\varepsilon_{r,\text{SiO}_2}=3.9\).

Energy bands and carriers

Syllabus: formation of energy bands in solids, energy bands in intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors, equilibrium carrier concentration, direct and indirect band-gap semiconductors.

Notes

  • Why bands form: when atoms come close in a crystal, each discrete atomic level splits into a band of closely spaced levels. The valence band and the conduction band are separated by the gap \(E_g\). At 300 K: Si 1.12 eV, Ge 0.66 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV.
  • Intrinsic: \(n=p=n_i\), with \(n_i^2=N_cN_v\,e^{-E_g/kT}\). For Si at 300 K most GATE questions use \(n_i=1.5\times10^{10}\text{ cm}^{-3}\). Always use the value given in the question.
  • Mass-action law at equilibrium: \(np=n_i^2\), for any doping.
  • Fermi–Dirac: \(f(E)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{(E-E_F)/kT}}\), and \(f(E_F)=\tfrac12\) at any temperature.
  • \(n=N_c\,e^{-(E_c-E_F)/kT}=n_i\,e^{(E_F-E_i)/kT}\) and \(p=n_i\,e^{(E_i-E_F)/kT}\).
  • n-type (donors P, As, Sb): \(n\approx N_D\), \(p=n_i^2/N_D\), \(E_F-E_i=kT\ln(N_D/n_i)\). p-type (acceptors B, Al, Ga): \(p\approx N_A\), \(E_i-E_F=kT\ln(N_A/n_i)\).
  • Both dopants present (charge neutrality \(n+N_A=p+N_D\)): \[n=\frac{N_D-N_A}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{N_D-N_A}{2}\right)^2+n_i^2}\]
  • Temperature regions: freeze-out (dopants not ionised) at very low \(T\), extrinsic (\(n\approx N_D\)) over the useful range, intrinsic (\(n_i\gt N_D\)) at high \(T\).
  • Direct gap (GaAs, InP, GaN): the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum are at the same crystal momentum, so an electron can drop and emit a photon. Indirect gap (Si, Ge): a phonon is also needed, so light emission is very inefficient.

Seen in real life

LEDs and laser diodes are made from GaAs, GaN and their alloys, never from silicon, because silicon’s indirect gap turns almost all recombination into heat. Electronics has a maximum junction temperature (often 125 to 175 °C for silicon) partly because \(n_i\) climbs steeply with temperature until leakage swamps the doping. A thermistor uses that same steep temperature dependence as a sensor.

Common traps

  • \(np=n_i^2\) holds only at thermal equilibrium, not under illumination or bias.
  • \(n_i\) differs between textbooks (\(1.0\times10^{10}\) or \(1.5\times10^{10}\) for Si). Use the question’s value.
  • \(E_i\) is not exactly at mid-gap unless the effective masses are equal. Most questions say to assume it is.
  • \(kT\) is 25.9 meV only at 300 K. Scale it if the temperature differs.

Practice

Silicon is doped with \(N_D=10^{16}\text{ cm}^{-3}\), \(n_i=1.5\times10^{10}\text{ cm}^{-3}\), \(T=300\text{ K}\). Find the hole concentration and \(E_F-E_i\).

\(p=\dfrac{n_i^2}{N_D}=\dfrac{2.25\times10^{20}}{10^{16}}=2.25\times10^4\text{ cm}^{-3}\).

\(E_F-E_i=0.0259\ln\dfrac{10^{16}}{1.5\times10^{10}}=0.0259\times13.41=0.347\text{ eV}\). The simulation opens on exactly these values.

Carrier transport

Syllabus: diffusion current, drift current, mobility and resistivity, generation and recombination of carriers, Poisson and continuity equations.

Notes

  • Drift: \(v_d=\mu E\). \(J_{drift}=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)E=\sigma E\), and \(\rho=1/\sigma\). Typical Si values at 300 K with light doping: \(\mu_n\approx1350\), \(\mu_p\approx480\text{ cm}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s})\).
  • Mobility falls as doping rises (impurity scattering) and as temperature rises in the normal range (lattice scattering, \(\mu\propto T^{-3/2}\)). At high field the velocity saturates near \(10^7\text{ cm/s}\) in Si.
  • Diffusion: \(J_n=qD_n\dfrac{dn}{dx}\) and \(J_p=-qD_p\dfrac{dp}{dx}\). Electrons diffusing down a gradient carry current up it, hence the opposite signs.
  • Einstein relation: \(\dfrac{D}{\mu}=\dfrac{kT}{q}\).
  • Recombination: under low-level injection, excess minority carriers decay as \(\delta p(t)=\delta p(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_p}\). The recombination rate is \(\delta p/\tau_p\).
  • Continuity equation (holes): \[\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac1q\frac{\partial J_p}{\partial x}+G-R\] In steady state with diffusion only: \(\delta p(x)=\delta p(0)\,e^{-x/L_p}\), where \(L_p=\sqrt{D_p\tau_p}\) is the diffusion length.
  • Poisson’s equation: \(\dfrac{dE}{dx}=\dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{q}{\varepsilon}(p-n+N_D-N_A)\), with \(E=-\dfrac{dV}{dx}\).
  • Hall effect: \(R_H=\dfrac{1}{qp}\) for p-type, \(-\dfrac{1}{qn}\) for n-type. The sign gives the carrier type, the magnitude gives the concentration, and \(\mu=\sigma|R_H|\).

Seen in real life

Hall-effect sensors are everywhere in a car: wheel-speed sensors for ABS, crankshaft and camshaft position sensors, and brushless-motor commutation. Each is a small semiconductor plate in which a magnetic field pushes drifting carriers sideways and produces a measurable voltage. Diffusion is the same process that spreads a drop of ink through still water.

Common traps

  • Mixing metres and centimetres. \(\mu\) in cm²/V·s needs \(n\) in cm⁻³ and \(E\) in V/cm.
  • The hole diffusion current has a minus sign. The electron diffusion current does not.
  • Conductivity is set almost entirely by the majority carrier. Do not forget this and add a negligible term with the wrong exponent.

Practice

n-type silicon has \(N_D=10^{16}\text{ cm}^{-3}\) and \(\mu_n=1350\text{ cm}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s})\). Find its resistivity.

\(\sigma=qN_D\mu_n=1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times1350=2.16\ (\Omega\cdot\text{cm})^{-1}\). \(\rho=0.463\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}\).

With \(\mu_n=1350\text{ cm}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s})\) and an electron lifetime of 1 µs at 300 K, find \(D_n\) and the diffusion length.

\(D_n=\mu_n\dfrac{kT}{q}=1350\times0.0259=35\text{ cm}^2/\text{s}\).

\(L_n=\sqrt{D_n\tau_n}=\sqrt{35\times10^{-6}}=5.9\times10^{-3}\text{ cm}=59\ \mu\text{m}\).

P-N junction and Zener

Syllabus: P-N junction, Zener diode.

Notes

  • Built-in potential: \(V_{bi}=\dfrac{kT}{q}\ln\dfrac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}\).
  • Depletion width (abrupt junction, applied voltage \(V\), negative for reverse bias): \[W=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A}+\frac{1}{N_D}\right)(V_{bi}-V)}\]
  • Charge balance \(N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n\), so the depletion region extends mainly into the lightly doped side. Peak field \(E_{max}=\dfrac{qN_Dx_n}{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{2(V_{bi}-V)}{W}\).
  • Junction (depletion) capacitance: \(C_j=\dfrac{\varepsilon A}{W}=\dfrac{C_{j0}}{(1-V/V_{bi})^m}\) with \(m=\tfrac12\) for an abrupt junction and \(\tfrac13\) for a linearly graded one. Dominates in reverse bias.
  • Diffusion capacitance: \(C_d=\dfrac{\tau I}{V_T}\). Dominates in forward bias.
  • Diode equation: \(I=I_s\big(e^{V/\eta V_T}-1\big)\), with \(I_s=qA\,n_i^2\left(\dfrac{D_p}{L_pN_D}+\dfrac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\right)\).
  • Small-signal resistance \(r_d=\dfrac{\eta V_T}{I}\). At constant current the forward voltage falls by about 2 mV per °C. \(I_s\) roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise.
  • Zener breakdown: tunnelling across a very thin depletion region, both sides heavily doped, below about 5 V, negative temperature coefficient. Avalanche breakdown: impact ionisation, lighter doping, above about 7 V, positive temperature coefficient.
  • Zener regulator: choose the series resistor so that the Zener current stays between \(I_{Z,min}\) and \(I_{Z,max}\) over the full range of input voltage and load current: \(R=\dfrac{V_{in}-V_Z}{I_Z+I_L}\).

Seen in real life

A varactor diode is a reverse-biased junction used as a voltage-controlled capacitor. It tunes the oscillator in every FM radio and phone. Zener and TVS diodes clamp voltage spikes on power and signal lines. The −2 mV/°C drift of a forward-biased junction is used as the temperature sensor inside processors and power modules.

Common traps

  • In \(W\), the term is \((V_{bi}-V)\). For 5 V reverse bias this is \(V_{bi}+5\).
  • Use \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\) with \(\varepsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-14}\text{ F/cm}\) when doping is in cm⁻³.
  • “60 mV per decade” of current applies to an ideal diode with \(\eta=1\) at room temperature.

Practice

A silicon junction has \(N_A=10^{17}\) and \(N_D=10^{15}\text{ cm}^{-3}\), with \(n_i=1.5\times10^{10}\). Find \(V_{bi}\) at 300 K. On which side is most of the depletion region?

\(V_{bi}=0.0259\ln\dfrac{10^{32}}{2.25\times10^{20}}=0.0259\times26.82=0.695\text{ V}\).

\(x_n/x_p=N_A/N_D=100\), so 99 % of the depletion region lies in the lightly doped n side.

An ideal diode carries 1 mA at 0.70 V. Find its small-signal resistance, and the current at 0.76 V.

\(r_d=V_T/I=25.9\ \Omega\).

\(I=1\text{ mA}\times e^{0.06/0.0259}=1\text{ mA}\times e^{2.32}\approx10\text{ mA}\). An extra 60 mV multiplies the current by ten.

BJT

Syllabus: BJT.

Notes

  • Modes: active (emitter junction forward, collector junction reverse), saturation (both forward), cut-off (both reverse), reverse active (roles swapped).
  • \(I_E=I_C+I_B\). \(I_C=\beta I_B=\alpha I_E\). \(\beta=\dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\) and \(\alpha=\dfrac{\beta}{1+\beta}\).
  • \(\alpha=\gamma\,\alpha_T\): emitter injection efficiency times base transport factor. For a high \(\beta\): dope the emitter far more heavily than the base (\(\gamma\to1\)) and make the base thin compared with the diffusion length, \(\alpha_T\approx1-\dfrac{W_B^2}{2L_B^2}\).
  • Doping order: emitter > base > collector. The collector has the largest area.
  • \(I_C=I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}\left(1+\dfrac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right)\). The second factor is the Early effect: a larger \(V_{CE}\) widens the collector depletion region and narrows the base.
  • Leakage: \(I_C=\beta I_B+(1+\beta)I_{CBO}\), so \(I_{CEO}=(1+\beta)I_{CBO}\).
  • Small-signal parameters: \(g_m=\dfrac{I_C}{V_T}\), \(r_\pi=\dfrac{\beta}{g_m}\), \(r_e=\dfrac{V_T}{I_E}\), \(r_o=\dfrac{V_A}{I_C}\), \(f_T=\dfrac{g_m}{2\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)}\).
  • In saturation \(V_{CE,sat}\approx0.2\text{ V}\) and \(I_C\lt\beta I_B\).

Seen in real life

An optocoupler is an LED facing a phototransistor in one package. It passes a signal with no electrical connection, which is how industrial controller inputs are isolated from field wiring. The difference between the \(V_{BE}\) of two BJTs run at different currents is exactly proportional to absolute temperature. Band-gap voltage references and chip temperature sensors are built on that fact.

Common traps

  • After assuming active mode, verify \(V_{CE}\gt0.2\text{ V}\). If not, the transistor is saturated and \(I_C=\beta I_B\) no longer holds.
  • A small change in \(\alpha\) is a large change in \(\beta\): \(\alpha=0.98\) gives \(\beta=49\), and \(\alpha=0.99\) gives \(\beta=99\).
  • For a pnp transistor every voltage polarity and current direction reverses.

Practice

A transistor has \(\alpha=0.98\) and \(I_{CBO}=1\ \mu\text{A}\). Find \(I_C\) for \(I_B=20\ \mu\text{A}\).

\(\beta=0.98/0.02=49\). \(I_C=49\times20\ \mu\text{A}+50\times1\ \mu\text{A}=0.98+0.05=1.03\text{ mA}\).

A BJT is biased at \(I_C=1\text{ mA}\) with \(\beta=100\) and \(V_A=100\text{ V}\). Find \(g_m\), \(r_\pi\) and \(r_o\) at 300 K.

\(g_m=\dfrac{1\text{ mA}}{25.9\text{ mV}}=38.6\text{ mS}\). \(r_\pi=\dfrac{100}{38.6\text{ mS}}=2.59\text{ k}\Omega\). \(r_o=\dfrac{100}{1\text{ mA}}=100\text{ k}\Omega\).

MOS capacitor and MOSFET

Syllabus: MOS capacitor, MOSFET, scaling in MOSFETs.

Notes

  • MOS capacitor on p-type silicon: accumulation for \(V_G\lt V_{FB}\) (holes at the surface), depletion just above \(V_{FB}\), inversion for \(V_G\gt V_T\) (an electron layer at the surface). Strong inversion begins when the surface potential reaches \(2\phi_F\), with \(\phi_F=\dfrac{kT}{q}\ln\dfrac{N_A}{n_i}\).
  • Threshold voltage: \[V_T=V_{FB}+2\phi_F+\frac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_sN_A(2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}},\qquad C_{ox}=\frac{\varepsilon_{ox}}{t_{ox}}\]
  • Body effect: \(V_T=V_{T0}+\gamma\big(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F}\big)\), \(\gamma=\dfrac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_sN_A}}{C_{ox}}\).
  • C–V curve: \(C_{ox}\) in accumulation, falling through depletion as \(C_{ox}\) in series with \(C_{dep}\). In inversion a low-frequency measurement returns to \(C_{ox}\), while a high-frequency measurement stays at the minimum.
  • MOSFET drain current with \(V_{ov}=V_{GS}-V_T\) and \(k_n=\mu_nC_{ox}\dfrac WL\): triode (\(V_{DS}\lt V_{ov}\)): \(I_D=k_n\big[V_{ov}V_{DS}-\tfrac12V_{DS}^2\big]\). Saturation (\(V_{DS}\ge V_{ov}\)): \(I_D=\tfrac12k_nV_{ov}^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})\).
  • In saturation: \(g_m=k_nV_{ov}=\sqrt{2k_nI_D}=\dfrac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}\) and \(r_o=\dfrac{1}{\lambda I_D}\). In deep triode the channel is a resistor \(\dfrac{1}{k_nV_{ov}}\).
  • pMOS uses holes, whose mobility is roughly 2 to 3 times lower, so pMOS devices are made wider in CMOS gates.
  • Short-channel effects: threshold roll-off, drain-induced barrier lowering, velocity saturation, hot carriers, and subthreshold leakage (at best 60 mV per decade at 300 K).

Seen in real life

A phone processor contains billions of MOSFETs used as switches, and decades of scaling is what made that possible. The same device at a much larger size, as a power MOSFET or IGBT, switches the current in motor drives, inverters and chargers. A touch-lamp dimmer and an analog multiplexer both use the deep-triode region, where the MOSFET is a voltage-controlled resistor.

Common traps

  • “Saturation” means opposite things: for a MOSFET it is the amplifying region, for a BJT it is the fully-on switch region.
  • Some books define \(k_n'=\mu_nC_{ox}\) without \(W/L\), and some absorb the \(\tfrac12\). Read the question’s definition.
  • Always check \(V_{DS}\) against \(V_{ov}\) before choosing a formula.
  • A source-to-body reverse bias raises \(V_T\). It never lowers it.

Practice

An nMOS has \(\mu_nC_{ox}=100\ \mu\text{A/V}^2\), \(W/L=10\), \(V_T=1\text{ V}\), \(\lambda=0\). Find \(I_D\) and \(g_m\) for \(V_{GS}=2\text{ V}\), \(V_{DS}=3\text{ V}\). Repeat for \(V_{DS}=0.5\text{ V}\).

\(k_n=1\text{ mA/V}^2\), \(V_{ov}=1\text{ V}\). For \(V_{DS}=3\text{ V}\ge V_{ov}\): saturation. \(I_D=\tfrac12(1)(1)^2=0.5\text{ mA}\) and \(g_m=k_nV_{ov}=1\text{ mS}\).

For \(V_{DS}=0.5\text{ V}\lt V_{ov}\): triode. \(I_D=1\times\big[(1)(0.5)-0.125\big]=0.375\text{ mA}\). Set these values in the simulation with \(\lambda=0\) to confirm.

LED, photodiode, solar cell

Syllabus: LED, photo diode and solar cell.

Notes

  • Photon energy: \(E=hf=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}\). In practical units \(\lambda(\mu\text{m})=\dfrac{1.24}{E(\text{eV})}\).
  • LED: a forward-biased junction in a direct-gap material. Injected carriers recombine and emit photons with energy close to \(E_g\), so the material sets the colour. GaAs emits infrared near 870 nm. GaN-based alloys emit blue, and a phosphor converts part of it to make white.
  • Photodiode: operated in reverse bias (third quadrant). Light with \(hf\ge E_g\) generates electron-hole pairs, adding a photocurrent \(I_{ph}\) to the reverse current. Cut-off wavelength \(\lambda_c=1.24/E_g\).
  • Responsivity: \(R=\dfrac{I_{ph}}{P_{opt}}=\eta\,\dfrac{q\lambda}{hc}=\dfrac{\eta\,\lambda(\mu\text{m})}{1.24}\text{ A/W}\), where \(\eta\) is the quantum efficiency.
  • A PIN photodiode has a wide intrinsic layer, giving high efficiency and low capacitance, hence speed. An avalanche photodiode adds internal gain.
  • Solar cell: the same junction with no external bias, delivering power in the fourth quadrant: \(I=I_L-I_s\big(e^{V/V_T}-1\big)\) with \(V_T=kT/q\). Short-circuit current \(I_{sc}=I_L\). Open-circuit voltage \(V_{oc}=V_T\ln\!\left(\dfrac{I_L}{I_s}+1\right)\).
  • Fill factor \(FF=\dfrac{V_mI_m}{V_{oc}I_{sc}}\) and efficiency \(\eta=\dfrac{V_mI_m}{P_{in}}\). \(I_{sc}\) is proportional to light intensity. \(V_{oc}\) grows only logarithmically with it.

Seen in real life

A TV remote is an infrared LED, and the TV’s receiver is a photodiode. An optical-fibre link is a laser at one end and a PIN photodiode at the other. Photoelectric sensors on a conveyor detect a part when it interrupts the beam between an LED and a photodiode. Rooftop solar inverters run a maximum power point tracker that keeps each panel at the peak of its power curve as the sunlight changes.

Common traps

  • A photodiode is reverse biased. A solar cell is unbiased and generates power. An LED is forward biased.
  • Silicon cannot detect wavelengths beyond about 1.1 µm, because those photons have less energy than its gap.
  • Responsivity rises with wavelength up to the cut-off, then drops to zero.

Practice

GaAs has \(E_g=1.42\text{ eV}\). What wavelength does a GaAs LED emit? A photodiode has a quantum efficiency of 0.8 at 0.87 µm. Find its responsivity.

\(\lambda=1.24/1.42=0.873\ \mu\text{m}\), in the near infrared.

\(R=0.8\times0.87/1.24=0.56\text{ A/W}\).

A solar cell has \(I_L=3\text{ A}\) and \(I_s=1\text{ nA}\) at 300 K. Find \(V_{oc}\).

\(V_{oc}=0.0259\ln(3\times10^9)=0.0259\times21.82=0.565\text{ V}\). The simulation uses these values at 1 sun.
